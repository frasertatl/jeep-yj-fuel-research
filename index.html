Fuel Starvation Diagnosis

Vehicle history & important qualification

Vehicle: 1987 Wrangler YJ, reported six-cylinder.
Problem: intermittent fuel starvation / apparent blockage; mechanical fuel pump suspected.
Previous work: tank flushed several years ago.

Engine identification still pending: later photos and video show an inline-six, carburetor, and a replacement mechanical pump, but do not conclusively establish whether the engine remains the original AMC 4.2L/258 or has been swapped. Confirm engine casting/stamping and compare removed pump to replacement before buying more parts.

Working hypothesis, not diagnosis: Weak pump, deteriorated or collapsing suction hose, clogged filter/pickup, a blocked vent, and vapor lock can all create similar intermittent symptoms. Use measured pressure, flow and inlet restriction to differentiate them.

Original-style fuel path

Tank + pickup sock→Supply line / hoses→Mechanical pump→3-port filter→Carburetor

The three-port fuel filter also has a small return/vapor line back toward the tank. Correct return routing matters, especially in hot weather. Verify the installation and the fuel-flow arrow against factory instructions.

Top potential faults

SuspectWhat to check
1. Mechanical fuel pumpDiaphragm/check valves, pump lever, and actual fuel pressure and delivered volume while symptom occurs.
2. Three-port filterClogging, wrong filter, reversed flow, or incorrect vapor-return orientation.
3. Rubber suction hoseInternally degraded or softened hose that collapses under pump suction; bends, kinks, old clamps.
4. Tank pickup / sockRestricted inlet despite earlier tank flushing; rust, new debris or failing strainer.
5. Tank ventilationRestricted venting can create a tank vacuum after some driving.
6. Vapor lockEngine stalls or starves only when hot / high altitude, especially after heat soak.

Diagnostic sequence: avoid unnecessary pump replacement

  1. Document symptoms. Hot or cold? After driving how long? Under load or idle? Restart immediately or only after cooling? Fuel level?
  2. Inspect external connections. Identify all short rubber sections between tank and pump; check hose rating, condition, clamps, nearby exhaust heat, leaks and kinks.
  3. Inspect/replace aged filter. Match the stock three-port arrangement and return routing. Verify the small return nipple orientation per factory service information.
  4. Test pump discharge. Use a proper low-pressure fuel gauge and factory pressure/volume procedure. Test during the failure if possible; do not rely on a generic PSI estimate.
  5. Check pump inlet restriction. Excessive suction vacuum points toward an upstream line, pickup or vent problem; low discharge with an unrestricted inlet points toward the pump or its drive.
  6. Investigate vapor lock. Correlate failures with hot weather/heat soak and inspect the OEM fuel return and routing. Review TSB 18-53-89.
  7. Inspect tank/pickup if tests indicate. Earlier flushing is helpful history, but is not proof the pickup is clear today.
Fuel safety: Gasoline and vapor can ignite. No open containers of running fuel, smoking, exposed sparks or testing near hot exhaust. Use appropriate fuel-rated fittings, eye protection, adequate ventilation and a fire extinguisher; have a mechanic perform pressure/flow tests where needed.
